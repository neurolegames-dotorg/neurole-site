## Where a memory is built

Long-term memory does not live in one place, but it is assembled in one. The
**hippocampus**, curled inside the medial temporal lobe, binds the scattered
fragments of an experience — what you saw, where you were, how it felt — into
something that can later be recalled as a single event.

{{brain: hippocampus | title=The hippocampus | camera=medial | cutaway=left | caption=Both hippocampi, seen from the midline with the left hemisphere removed.}}

Damage here does not erase the past. It stops new memories from forming, which
is a different and stranger deficit: the patient can hold a conversation, but
cannot remember having had it.

## The loop it sits in

The hippocampus is one station on a circuit. Fibres leave it through the
**fornix**, arc forward to the **mamillary bodies**, relay up to the anterior
nucleus of the **thalamus**, and reach the **cingulate gyrus** before returning
to the hippocampus by way of the parahippocampal cortex.

{{brain: papez circuit | title=The Papez circuit | camera=medial | cutaway=left | button=Trace the whole circuit in 3D}}

> Break the loop anywhere and memory suffers — which is why thiamine deficiency,
> which damages the mamillary bodies, produces amnesia at all.

## Why the shape matters

Reading that list is one thing. Seeing that the fornix genuinely arches over
the thalamus, and that the mamillary bodies sit directly beneath it, is what
makes the circuit stop being a diagram and start being an object.

- The hippocampus is longer than most people picture it
- The fornix is a single dense bundle, not a diffuse projection
- The mamillary bodies are tiny — a pair of structures a few millimetres across

Turn it around and the relationship is obvious in a way no cross-section
manages.
